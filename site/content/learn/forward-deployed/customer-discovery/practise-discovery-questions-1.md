# Practise Discovery Questions


## How to use this practice

Customer-scenario rounds ask questions like these, often in role-play. For each one:

1. Answer aloud in two minutes.
2. Open the outline and note what you missed.
3. Answer the follow-up too, because interviewers usually push once.

Open-problem breakdowns are in [practise decomposition interview cases](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/practise-decomposition-interview-cases). All customers and figures are fictional.

> **What you'll practise**
>
> - Planning discovery and finding the problem behind a request.
> - Asking without leading, and observing the work.
> - Handling stakeholders, owners, approvals and workflow maps.

## Planning discovery

Taught in [the discovery conversation](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/the-discovery-conversation).

<details>
<summary>Practice question 1. What is the difference between business discovery and technical discovery, and which comes first?</summary>

- **Answer:** Business discovery asks who has the problem, what it costs, what a good outcome looks like and who decides. Technical discovery asks which systems, data, access and constraints exist. Business comes first, because it tells you which technical questions matter.
- **Trade-off:** Early technical work saves calendar time, but before the outcome is clear it targets the wrong systems.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "IT is the first team you meet." Ask who the business owner is and book them next.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 2. Plan the agenda for a first two-hour discovery workshop.</summary>

- **Answer:** Ten minutes on purpose. Thirty with the sponsor on cost, goal and deadline. Forty with users, walking one real case. Twenty with IT on systems and data. Ten on security, legal and budget constraints. Ten on next steps and owners, including an observation session.
- **Trade-off:** A packed agenda rushes the workflow walk. Protect the forty minutes with users.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "The sponsor leaves after twenty minutes." Get the goal and decision rights first.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 3. A customer opens with "We want an AI assistant for our sales team." What do you ask first?</summary>

- **Answer:** What should change for the sales team if this works, and how they would measure it. Then which part of the sales day is slow or error-prone, and who feels it. Then what they have tried. The answers turn a named solution into a problem, a metric and users.
- **Trade-off:** Questioning the request can feel like resistance. Say the questions protect their budget.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "They insist on the assistant." Scope one around the problem the answers revealed.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 4. What clarifying questions do you ask before designing anything?</summary>

- **Answer:** Five groups. Outcome: what changes and how it is measured. Users: who, how many, what they do now. Constraints: deadline, budget, security, compliance. Data: what exists and who owns it. The rollout: who approves, who supports it. Outcome questions go first.
- **Trade-off:** Too many questions stall the meeting. Too few design for the wrong problem.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "You get only three questions." Outcome and metric, current users and their pain, and the hardest constraint.

</details>

## Finding the problem behind the request

Taught in [the discovery conversation](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/the-discovery-conversation) and [choosing the first use case](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/choosing-the-first-use-case).

<details>
<summary>Practice question 5. The customer hands you a list of twelve features. You suspect one change would fix most of the pain. How do you handle it?</summary>

- **Answer:** Group the features by the problem each solves. Most often share one root cause, such as a missing field or a slow approval. Propose the one change with its expected effect, and keep the list as the backlog.
- **Trade-off:** Setting the list aside can feel dismissive. Building all twelve spreads the budget over symptoms.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Each feature has an executive behind it." Show the problem each serves and let the sponsor rank them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 6. Describe a time discovery changed the problem you set out to solve.</summary>

- **Answer:** Use a real case in four parts: what was asked (faster document entry), what discovery showed (approval waits caused most of the delay), what changed and who agreed, and the result with a number.
- **Trade-off:** Overriding the customer sounds arrogant. Show that they made the call with your evidence.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "What if you had been wrong?" The baseline and agreed metric would have shown it.

</details>

## Asking without leading

Taught in [the discovery conversation](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/the-discovery-conversation).

<details>
<summary>Practice question 7. How do you run a discovery session without leading the customer to your answer?</summary>

- **Answer:** Ask about past events, not opinions: "Walk me through the last case you handled." Use open questions and never name a solution. Let silences run. Ask for examples and numbers. Keep design ideas out of the room until the problem is agreed.
- **Trade-off:** Open questions are slower. Leading questions get quick agreement to the wrong thing.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "What would you build?" Promise a proposal once you have seen the work, with a date.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 8. Give five interview questions that surface workarounds.</summary>

- **Answer:** "What do you keep outside the system to get this done?" "When did a case last get stuck, and what did you do?" "Who do you message when information is missing?" "What do new starters get wrong in their first month?" "What would break if you were off for two weeks?"
- **Trade-off:** Staff may fear blame. Say you are studying the process, not their performance.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "They say there are no workarounds." Ask to see their screen during a real case.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 9. What questions do you ask a daily user, a business owner and a system administrator?</summary>

- **Answer:** User: walk me through your last case, where do you wait, what do you check twice. Business owner: what does this cost, what result justifies funding, who else must agree. Administrator: which systems and data are involved, who grants access, how long changes take to approve.
- **Trade-off:** Separate sessions take longer. Mixing roles in one room makes users quieter.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Their answers conflict." Conflicts show where the process differs from the description.

</details>

## Watching the work

Taught in [observing the work](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/observing-the-work).

<details>
<summary>Practice question 10. How do you learn how a process really runs, including its exceptions?</summary>

- **Answer:** Interview for the described process. Sit with each role on their live queue, noting when cases start, wait, move and come back. Then read the system records to see how often each pattern happens. Exceptions are the cases people park, forward or handle by phone.
- **Trade-off:** This takes days, not hours. A process built from the description alone misses the costly cases.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "There is no system data." Keep a tally sheet with the team for a week.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 11. What does each stakeholder group know that the others do not?</summary>

- **Answer:** Sponsors: goal, budget and politics. Process owners: the official process and its targets. Users: workarounds, waits and exceptions. Control owners: which steps rules require. System owners: data, access and change rules.
- **Trade-off:** Meeting every group costs time. Missing one costs a late surprise.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Which group do teams skip?" Control owners, and frontline users when access is guarded.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 12. Design a half-day observation exercise for a claims team.</summary>

- **Answer:** Brief the team on purpose and confidentiality. Sit with two handlers for ninety minutes each on live claims, noting timestamps, waits, handoffs and side tools. Spend thirty minutes with the lead on what a bad week looks like. Collect checklists and templates, then compare notes with a week of claim timestamps.
- **Trade-off:** Two people is a small sample: it shows what to measure, and system data shows how often.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Staff are nervous." Share your notes afterwards, without names.

</details>

## Disagreement and ownership

Taught in [stakeholder and workflow maps](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/stakeholder-and-workflow-maps).

<details>
<summary>Practice question 13. Two senior stakeholders want opposite things from the project, and both outrank your sponsor's deputy. What do you do?</summary>

- **Answer:** Meet each separately to learn what they need and why. Write the conflict down neutrally: each position, its reason and each option's cost. Ask the sponsor, who owns the decision, to settle it with both in the room. Record the decision.
- **Trade-off:** Escalating may feel political. Picking a side decides something that is not yours to decide.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "The sponsor will not decide." Say what work stops until they do, and set a date.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 14. The sponsor, the compliance team and the users each want something different from the same workflow. How do you reach a design?</summary>

- **Answer:** Trace each requirement to its reason. Compliance backed by law or contract is a constraint, the sponsor's goal is the target, and user needs decide adoption. Design within the constraints to meet the goal, then shape it around users.
- **Trade-off:** Full compliance can add steps users dislike. Make those steps cheap, such as pre-filled evidence, rather than arguing them away.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Compliance's rule is a habit, not a law." Then it can be traded, but the compliance lead decides.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 15. Nobody owns the process you are improving. Each team owns one step. What do you do?</summary>

- **Answer:** Map steps and handoffs with each team's lead so the gaps show. Raise the missing owner with the sponsor, propose an interim owner for the engagement, and make a permanent owner part of the handover.
- **Trade-off:** Raising it may look like overreach. Delivering into an unowned process means nobody keeps it running.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Should you be the owner?" Only for the engagement. A permanent owner must sit with the customer.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 16. Describe how you documented and resolved an ownership conflict between teams.</summary>

- **Answer:** Use a real case: the activity both teams claimed or avoided, how you found it and its impact. Then the write-up (each team's view, the options), who decided and what changed, such as an on-call rota or a runbook support boundary.
- **Trade-off:** Conflict stories risk blaming a team. Focus on the gap in the process.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "What would you do differently?" Map owners before the first incident.

</details>

## Approvals and controls

Taught in [stakeholder and workflow maps](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/stakeholder-and-workflow-maps).

<details>
<summary>Practice question 17. During observation, you see staff routinely skip an approval step to meet deadlines. What do you do?</summary>

- **Answer:** Do not report individuals. Find why the step exists and who owns its rule. Measure how often it is skipped and why: a slow approver, a pointless step or an impossible deadline. Take the evidence to the control owner and sponsor with options, such as a faster path or a changed threshold.
- **Trade-off:** Raising it may expose a compliance gap. Ignoring it builds on a process that is not what it claims.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Should your system enforce the step?" Only once the step is workable. Otherwise people find a new way around it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 18. An approval step adds two days, and the approver always says yes. The sponsor wants it gone. What do you do?</summary>

- **Answer:** Ask the control owner which rule requires it and what the approver checks. If the rule is real, keep the step and make it cheaper: prepare the evidence automatically, batch approvals or agree a service level. If nobody can name a rule, the control owner decides whether it goes.
- **Trade-off:** Keeping the step costs time. Removing a mandated control can cost a fine or an audit finding.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "It always says yes, so it adds nothing." It may be the auditor's evidence that someone checked.

</details>

## Maps

Taught in [stakeholder and workflow maps](/learn/forward-deployed/customer-discovery/stakeholder-and-workflow-maps).

<details>
<summary>Practice question 19. Map a workflow that crosses a CRM, an ERP and email. How do you go about it?</summary>

- **Answer:** Trace three real cases end to end: simple, large and an exception. Draw swimlanes per team, marking which system each step reads and writes. Mark handoffs, especially email ones, which leave no record. Note waits and exceptions with their frequency, and name an owner per step.
- **Trade-off:** Tracing real cases is slower, but it catches the email steps nobody mentions.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Where do things go wrong?" At the email handoffs, which no system tracks.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 20. Build a system and ownership map for a new engagement. What goes on it?</summary>

- **Answer:** Each system, its data, the fields it is the system of record for, its owner and who grants access. The connections and which way data moves. For each step, the system used and the person accountable. Mark the gaps: unowned fields, manual copies and missing access.
- **Trade-off:** A full map takes a week. A partial one with marked gaps is enough to start.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Two systems both claim a field." Name the one that records the real event, and make the other read from it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 21. Produce a stakeholder map for a hospital discharge project. Who is on it?</summary>

- **Answer:** Sponsor: the chief operating officer. Process owner: the head of patient flow. Users: ward nurses, discharge coordinators, pharmacists. Control owners: clinical and information governance. System owners: patient record and bed management teams. Affected: patients, social care, transport. Note what each can approve or block.
- **Trade-off:** A large map is harder to manage. Missing pharmacy or social care stalls discharges you sped up elsewhere.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "Who has the most informal power?" Often senior nurses, because their adoption decides whether it works.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Practice question 22. Present a current and proposed workflow to the customer's leadership. How do you show it?</summary>

- **Answer:** Use the same swimlanes for both: the current map with measured waits and exceptions, then the proposed map with only the changes highlighted. For each change, show what it removes, the lane it affects and the expected effect on their metric.
- **Trade-off:** A detailed map overwhelms leaders. Show one page and keep the detail in an appendix.
- **Follow-up to expect:** "A leader objects to a change in their lane." Ask what it breaks, then adjust or escalate to the sponsor.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Turn a requested solution into a problem, a metric and a user.
- Ask about real past cases, then size what you saw with system data.
- Let the person who owns a decision make it, and find the rule behind any approval.
- Show current and proposed workflows on the same lanes.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Why ask "walk me through the last case" instead of "how does the process work"?</summary>

A real past case brings out the waits, workarounds and exceptions. A general question gets the official process.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why should your system not enforce a skipped approval step straight away?</summary>

If the step is unworkable, people will find a new way around it. Fix why it is skipped first.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why are email handoffs the first place to look on a multi-system map?</summary>

No system records them, so cases wait or get lost there without anyone seeing.

</details>
